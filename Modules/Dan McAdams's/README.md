Dan McAdams's life-story model offers a rigorous, empirically grounded framework for understanding identity as an internalized and evolving narrative—one that maps remarkably well onto Muse's generational memory and moral simulation architecture.

Core Theoretical Framework

McAdams's model, first articulated in 1985 and elaborated across subsequent decades, contends that identity is an internalized and evolving self-story that draws together past, present, and future anticipations to form a unifying narrative of self that gives meaning and purpose. This is not merely a metaphor for identity—the constructed life story is the person's identity.

The model is rooted in Tomkins's script theory, which proposes that emotionally laden scenes in one's life are intensified through internal rehearsing and repetition to become overarching schemas for comprehending interpersonal interactions. These schemas become the templates that influence how we interact and make meaning.

A crucial insight for Muse's generative-memory architecture: McAdams describes narrative identity as "a psychosocial construction, a joint product of individual and society". The life story is coauthored by the individual and the cultural context in which they are lodged. This means the stories we hear about ourselves from others, as well as larger social narratives, greatly influence our identity—a principle directly applicable to tracking how a 1965 decision ripples into 2026.

Key Concepts and Mechanisms

Redemption and Contamination Sequences

McAdams identifies two master narrative patterns that organize how individuals interpret pivotal life events:

Redemption sequences occur when a story moves from a negative or difficult experience to a positive outcome: "the bad is redeemed, salvaged, mitigated, or made better in light of the ensuing good". These sequences are strongly associated with generativity, psychological well-being, and the capacity to find meaning in suffering.

Contamination sequences occur when something good is spoiled, ruined, or undermined by what follows: a positive beginning spirals into a negative conclusion. These predict lower levels of well-being and are more common in narratives of low points and adolescent events.

McAdams's research shows that highly generative adults—those concerned with guiding and contributing to the next generation—tend to construct redemptive narratives. This connects directly to Muse's generational memory feature: the question of whether a family line narrates its past as redemption or contamination becomes an identity-shaping force across decades.

The Life Story Interview

McAdams developed a structured interview protocol that asks participants to "picture your life as if it were a book or a novel" and divide it into chapters, typically between two and seven. The interview proceeds through key scenes (high points, low points, turning points), challenges, personal ideology, and future chapters. The full protocol has 22 segments and can take three to five hours.

For Muse's purposes, this methodology demonstrates how to elicit narrative structure without imposing it—the interviewer asks for chapters, but the participant determines their number and content. This aligns with Muse's stance of being a structured mirror rather than a plot generator.

The Three-Level Model of Personality

McAdams situates narrative identity within a broader architecture of personality as three layers:

· Level 1: Dispositional Traits — the social actor's broad, decontextualized tendencies (extraversion, neuroticism, agreeableness)
· Level 2: Characteristic Adaptations — the motivated agent's goals, values, motives, and strategies that are socially and temporally contextualized
· Level 3: Integrative Life Stories — the autobiographical author's internalized and evolving narrative that provides unity and purpose

This hierarchy is directly relevant to Muse's character-state schema. A character's traits (Level 1) are relatively stable, but their goals and values (Level 2) shift with context, and their narrative identity (Level 3) is the integrative story they tell about who they are and how they came to be that person. The moral simulation room would need to track all three levels, because a choice that violates Level 1 traits might be psychologically implausible, while a choice that contradicts Level 3 narrative identity creates an identity revision crisis.

Developmental Trajectory

McAdams's model is developmental but not stage-locked. Children as young as one to two years old comprehend others as intentional agents, and autobiographical memory emerges around age two. By their third birthday, children can co-construct past experiences in conversation and appreciate standard story features.

However, it is only during late adolescence and early adulthood that cognitive and psychosocial developments enable the individual to integrate their life into a comprehensible, culturally meaningful story. Crucially, unlike Erikson's stage model, McAdams argues that identity work is not confined to a particular period but is continually reworked throughout adult life.

This has profound implications for Muse's 1965–2026 timeline. A character in 1965 may be in the midst of forming their narrative identity, while the same character in 2026 is revising it in light of new information—and the generational memory engine can track how these revisions cascade.

Critiques and Limitations

McAdams himself has acknowledged potential problems with the redemptive narrative, particularly its cultural specificity and its risks of self-righteousness and American exceptionalism. The model has also been critiqued for assuming a high degree of self-awareness in the consolidating of identity, and for its emphasis on a singular, unifying story that may not account for the multiplicity of selfhood emphasized by dialogical self theorists like Hubert Hermans.

These critiques are productive for Muse. The engine should not assume that a character's narrative identity is coherent or unified—it may be fragmented, contested, or in active conflict with the narratives imposed by others. Hermans's dialogical self theory, which views identity as negotiated in inner dialogue between multiple I-positions, offers a complementary framework for modeling internal character disagreement.
